# Product Philosophy

> A visual system for turning intended time into lived memory.

Most productivity tools record what people *intend* to do. Journals record memories
*after* they happen. Evidence of Life connects planning, lived time, photos, places,
and memory in one private system — so the day you planned and the day you actually
lived stay side by side.

## The five layers

Evidence of Life is built from five layers that answer five different questions:

| Layer | Question it answers | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lists** | What needs to be done? | Capture tasks, habits, dues, goals, and intentions. |
| **Timeline** | When can it realistically happen? | Turn abstract tasks into visible time blocks. |
| **Actual activity** | What really happened? | Preserve completed, delayed, moved, interrupted, and unfinished activity. |
| **Map** | Where did life happen? | Connect photos, moments, activities, and memories to places. |
| **Recap** | Where did time go? | Understand the difference between intention and lived experience. |

In one line:

- Lists show **what**.
- Timelines reveal **when**.
- Actual records show **where time went**.
- Maps preserve **where life happened**.

## How a task becomes a memory

The core flow moves an intention through time and into a durable record:

1. A task or intention is captured.
2. It is dragged into the planned timeline.
3. It is adjusted as the day changes — moved, resized, deferred, or left unfinished.
4. It is converted into actual activity.
5. It is connected with a note, photo, focus session, or location.
6. It is revisited later through recap, calendar, and map.

Nothing in this flow requires a "perfect" day. Moved, partial, and abandoned work
all remain part of the record.

## Design principles

Evidence of Life is designed around challenges commonly experienced by people with
ADHD and time-blindness. It is **not** a diagnostic, clinical, or treatment tool.
These are design goals, not medical claims.

### 1. Externalize time
Make time visible instead of leaving it to internal estimation: a daily timeline,
visible task durations, planned-versus-actual comparison, focus-session history, and
calendar integration.

### 2. Reduce activation cost
Lower the energy required to start: drag tasks directly into time, minimal steps to
reschedule, quick capture, short focus sessions, and a clear next action.

### 3. Make small progress visible
Value partial work: short task durations, partial completion, progress retention,
visible open time gaps, and no requirement for a perfectly completed day.

### 4. Use context as a cue
Lean on recognition over recall: photos, locations, calendar events, focus sessions,
tags, and time of day all act as memory cues.

### 5. Preserve reality over perfection
Keep the truth of the day: retain original plans, record moved tasks, show
unexpected activities, keep unfinished work, and compare intention with reality
without judgment.

### 6. Lower memory burden
Reduce the effort of reconstructing a day: in-the-moment capture, automatic
contextual metadata, map-linked moments, calendar-assisted recap, and
evidence-based day reconstruction.

## The map as spatial memory

The map is not a travel-tracking feature or a wall of decorative pins. It is a
**spatial memory layer**: memories do not live only in dates — they also live in
places. A neighborhood shows detailed moments; a city shows where daily life
concentrates; the globe shows long-term traces. Selecting a place reveals its photo,
date, note, linked activity, and time spent.

We deliberately avoid describing the map as mere "visited places," implying constant
surveillance, or presenting it as a treatment feature.

## What this is not

- Not only a todo list — the timeline and map are central, not add-ons.
- Not a social feed — it is a private record, self-hostable, with no pressure to
  perform life publicly.
- Not a medical product — ADHD-friendly by design, but never diagnostic or
  therapeutic.
